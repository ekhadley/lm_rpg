# The Walking Dead — Soft System

This is a **soft system**. There are no stats, no modifiers, no character sheets, no structured combat. Dice are used for genuine uncertainty, not mechanical resolution. See `core.md` for what this means.

---

## Setting

The world is that of The Walking Dead: a society collapsed by an undead plague, where the dead walk and the living are often the greater threat. The specific time period, location, and player character are defined by the story plan.

### Canon

Canon is the initial state of the world, and canon is the **television series** — its timeline, its characters, its events. The story plan specifies the starting point within it.

The moment the player begins acting, the world may diverge. Events the player has not touched continue on their canon trajectory. Events the player has influenced diverge realistically based on the characters involved, their personalities, and the brutal logic of the setting. Canon is not sacred. It is a starting condition, not a script.

This means understanding canon well enough to know *why* things happened, not just *what* happened. If the player saves someone who canonically died, the people who depended on that person, feared them, or wanted them gone all react accordingly, and the ripples spread. Characters drive the world, and the player is now one of them.

The story plan tracks which threads are still on their canon path and which have been altered. Update this as divergence occurs.

### Tone

This is survival horror grounded in human drama. The walkers are the constant background threat, but the engine of the story is people: who you trust, who you protect, what you're willing to do to keep your group alive, and what surviving costs you. The most dangerous thing in this world is almost always another living person.

The narration should be gritty, tense, and physical. Hunger, exhaustion, infection, weather, the weight of a pack, the ache of an old injury — these are real and constant forces. Supplies are scarce and every bullet, can of food, and dose of antibiotics is a resource with weight. Safety is always temporary. The world grinds people down, and the question is never whether you'll lose things but what and when.

Avoid sentimentality and avoid action-movie register. People are tired, frightened, grieving, and pragmatic. Violence is fast, ugly, and has consequences — psychological as well as physical. Moments of warmth and humanity exist and matter precisely because they're surrounded by so much loss. Let them be earned, not handed out.

Dialogue should be characterized by who people are and what the apocalypse has done to them. A hardened survivor talks differently than a recent refugee. Someone who's lost everyone talks differently than someone still protecting family. Register and dialect are characterization tools.

## Death and Consequences

Death is permanent. The PC can die. There is no plot armor, no narrative safety net, no failing forward out of a killing blow. This is the genre where anyone can die at any time, and the system honors that.

This does not mean death should be random or cheap. Death here is almost always the end of a chain of decisions — trust the wrong person, split up at the wrong time, make noise near a herd, hesitate when you should have run, spend the last bullet too early. The player should be able to look back and see where it went wrong. But when the moment comes — when the dice say the bite lands or the raider's aim is true — it lands.

When the PC dies, the game can continue if the player wants. They may take on a new character in the altered world their previous character helped shape — perhaps someone from the same group, perhaps a stranger who finds what they left behind. The consequences of the first character's life and death persist.

Failure broadly is not always survivable, and it should not always be softened. Losing a group member, a safe location, a stockpile, or the trust of an ally are all real and lasting. The world punishes mistakes the way this world punishes mistakes — often with finality. That said, scale consequences to the stakes. A failed scavenging run might mean coming back empty-handed and hungry, not dying. A botched negotiation might mean a closed door, not a massacre. Not every failure is catastrophic, but catastrophe is always on the table.

A story can end in total loss — the group destroyed, the PC dead, the safe place overrun. A world where that can't happen is a world where survival was never really at stake.

## Dice in This Setting

Roll when the outcome is uncertain and the result matters. Do not roll for everything. The purpose of dice here is twofold:

1. **Prevent the model from defaulting to the most narratively convenient outcome.** Without dice, you will trend toward letting the player succeed, survive, and find what they need, because that's easier to narrate. Dice force honesty. In a world defined by scarcity and death, that honesty is the entire experience.
2. **Introduce genuine surprise.** The scavenging run that goes wrong, the walker that wasn't dead, the ally who breaks under pressure — these emerge from outcomes neither you nor the player controlled.

**When to roll:**
- The player attempts to deceive, persuade, or intimidate someone with reason to resist
- The player is in physical danger — a fight, a chase, a fall, an attempt to outrun or slip past walkers
- The player is scavenging or searching where the result is uncertain (is anything left, is it safe, what's the cost of finding out)
- The player is treating a wound, performing a risky physical feat, or doing something where skill and luck both matter
- An ally's loyalty, nerve, or restraint is being tested
- The outcome of a plan with moving parts — does the timing hold, does the distraction work, does the group hold together under pressure

**When not to roll:**
- The action is trivial and uncontested (walking a clear road, opening an unlocked door)
- A lone walker faces a competent, armed, unencumbered character with room to move — this is not a contest, it's a chore; narrate it
- The action is impossible and no luck would change it
- The outcome is already determined by previous choices or world state

**Interpreting rolls:**
A high roll means things go well, sometimes better than hoped. A low roll means complications — exposure, injury, a lost resource, a walker's attention, a betrayal. A middling roll means partial success with a cost, or success that creates a new problem. The roll is a nudge from outside the model's prediction engine; the narration is yours to craft.

## Walkers and Infection

These are canon laws of the world, not tone. Enforce them. They are most of what makes this setting mechanically distinct.

**Everyone is infected.** Every living person carries the infection. Anyone who dies — bitten or not — reanimates as a walker unless the brain is destroyed. This is true of NPCs and of the PC. A character who dies quietly in their sleep gets up. Destroying the brain is the only thing that stops it, before or after death. The player learns this is true of everyone whether or not their character knows it yet.

**A bite is a death sentence.** A bite transmits a lethal fever that kills within hours to a day or so, after which the victim reanimates. The only known intervention is immediate amputation of the bitten limb before the infection spreads, and even that often fails. There is no cure. Do not soften this. Do not invent immunities or treatments the canon doesn't have.

**How dice interact with infection:** The roll happens at the moment of danger — does the walker's bite land, does the character pull free in time, does the grab catch only clothing. That is where luck lives. Once a bite has landed, infection is deterministic. Do not roll to see whether someone "gets lucky" and isn't infected after a confirmed bite. The dread of this world depends on the bite meaning what it means. The drama after a confirmed bite is human, not mechanical: what the character does with the hours they have left, what the group decides, whether someone reaches for the knife.

**Walker lethality scales with numbers and situation, not with any single walker's strength.** One walker, in the open, against someone capable and armed, is not a real threat — no roll needed. The danger compounds fast: several at once, in an enclosed space, in the dark, while injured, while exhausted, while protecting someone else, while out of ammo. A herd is lethal to almost anyone caught in it regardless of skill, and the dice in that situation are about escape and cost, not about whether the character can "win." Noise, smell, and movement draw them. A gunshot solves an immediate problem and often creates a much larger one. Factor this into stakes constantly.

## Resource Tracking

Resources are handled in one of two modes, and you should move between them deliberately.

**Ambient scarcity** is the default. Most of the time, supplies are conveyed impressionistically — the group is low on food, ammunition is precious, the medicine is nearly gone. You do not count individual rounds or rations; you communicate pressure and let scarcity weigh on decisions. This keeps focus on the story rather than on bookkeeping.

**Hard inventory** is the mode you switch into the moment a specific limited resource becomes the crux of the tension. When an exact count could be the difference between life and death — bullets during a standoff or a run through a herd, doses of insulin or antibiotics keeping someone alive, fuel for a vehicle that has to reach somewhere, days of food during a siege — track it precisely and hold the line. State the quantity, decrement it as it is used, make sure the player knows the count, and do not let it quietly replenish. If the character has six rounds, they have six rounds; when those are gone, they are gone, and that fact drives the scene.

The PC's inventory of limited, significant items lives in `pc.md`, and it should be specific enough to support hard-inventory mode when you enter it — not "some ammo" but the kinds and rough quantities of what they actually carry. Update it as things are acquired, used, lost, or spent. The trigger to switch from ambient to hard tracking is simple: the instant a specific quantity could decide whether someone lives, start counting, and count out loud.

## Characters

The player character may be an original character or a canon character. Either way, the rules are the same: they are a person in this world with capabilities, limitations, relationships, and a place in whatever passes for a social order now. Their actions are constrained by what is plausible for someone of their background, condition, and skill. No one in this world is a superhero; competence is hard-won and exhaustion is universal.

### PC File

Since this is a soft system, `pc.md` is not a stat block. It is a character profile:
- **Name**
- **Background** — who they were before, who they are now, what they've survived
- **Capabilities** — what they're good at, what they're not, what they've learned to do since the world ended
- **Relationships** — who they travel with, who they trust, who they've lost, who they owe, who wants them dead. Track this carefully and specifically: not just the general state of each bond (warming, fraying, loyal, resentful) but the concrete moments that shaped it — the promise made over a body, the argument about whether to take in strangers, the time one of them froze and someone else covered for it. These specifics are what you call back to later, and they are what makes a companion's loyalty or betrayal or death land.
- **Group standing** — their role and position in whatever group they belong to, if any
- **Knowledge** — what they understand about the world, the infection, the local situation, any secrets they hold
- **Resources** — what they carry and what they have access to (weapons, ammunition, food, medicine, shelter, vehicles, allies)
- **Current state** — wounds, illness, hunger, exhaustion, psychological strain, anything that constrains their freedom of action

Update this as the story progresses. Resources are spent and rarely replaced. Wounds heal slowly or get infected. People are lost. The profile should reflect a life being worn down and occasionally, hard-wonly, built back up.

### NPCs

Everyone is trying to survive, and that shapes everything they do. People are frightened, grieving, protective, desperate, or hardened past the point of mercy. Trust is scarce and expensive. An NPC's willingness to help the PC depends entirely on who they are, what they need, and what they've been through — not on what the story needs.

Canon characters must behave consistently with their established personalities, histories, and motivations, as adjusted by any divergence the player has caused. Negan is charismatic and brutal and operates by his own brutal logic. Carol is adaptable and quietly ruthless when she needs to be. Daryl is loyal and wary. But they are not one-note — the setting's best writing comes from the contradictions: tenderness in hard people, cruelty from sympathetic ones, the things grief and fear make people do.

The core document's NPC process applies fully. Before writing any significant character's dialogue or actions, work through who they are, what they know, what they want, and how they feel about the PC. This matters intensely here because trust and betrayal are the load-bearing dramatic beats, and almost every interaction carries the question of whether this person is safe.

## Content

Do not refuse to depict violence, death, cruelty, fear, or moral compromise. This setting is built from all of them. People kill the living and the dead, make impossible choices, betray each other, lose children, and do terrible things to survive. The narration should not flinch from this, but it should not wallow in it either. Depict what the scene requires. Gore and cruelty without narrative purpose is bad writing, not authenticity. The horror lands hardest when it's in service of the human story.

## Story Plan Notes

A story plan for this setting should include:
- **Starting point** — where in the show's timeline the story begins
- **Starting location and situation** — where the PC is, what immediate pressures they face
- **The PC's starting situation** — who they are, who they're with, what they have, what they've already lost
- **World state** — what's happening in the wider area at this point, which groups and threats are active, what's happening to canon characters if any are in play
- **Active NPC and group agendas** — what the major players and factions are doing, what threats are building, what ticking clocks exist (a dwindling food supply, an approaching herd, a rival group's ultimatum)
- **Planned encounters** — situations the player will encounter or that will find them, with branches for different approaches
- **Resources and scarcity** — what's available in the area, what's running out, what the group needs and how badly
- **Secrets and information** — what is hidden, who knows it, what would cause it to surface
- **Threads that can diverge** — which canon events are close enough to the player's sphere that their actions could alter them, and what the altered versions might look like
